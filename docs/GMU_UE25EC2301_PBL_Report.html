4.1 Curve Model 1: Straight Line Fit ($y = ax + b$)

The principle of least squares minimizes the residual sum $\sum [y_i - (ax_i + b)]^2$. Setting partial derivatives to zero yields the standard normal equations:

(1) $\sum y = a\sum x + nb \quad \implies \quad 21.0a + 6b = 61.6$
(2) $\sum xy = a\sum x^2 + b\sum x \quad \implies \quad 91.0a + 21.0b = 283.3$

Solving simultaneously via Cramer's Rule: $\Delta = (6)(91) - (21)^2 = 546 - 441 = 105$.

$a = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x\sum y}{\Delta} = \frac{6(283.3) - (21.0)(61.6)}{105} = \frac{1699.8 - 1293.6}{105} = \mathbf{3.8686}$
$b = \frac{\sum y\sum x^2 - \sum x\sum xy}{\Delta} = \frac{(61.6)(91) - (21.0)(283.3)}{105} = \frac{5605.6 - 5949.3}{105} = \mathbf{-3.2733}$
Optimal Linear Fit: $y = 3.8686x - 3.2733 \quad$ (Residual Sum of Squares $\text{RSS} = 13.4110$)

4.2 Curve Model 2: Second-Degree Parabola Fit ($y = ax^2 + bx + c$)

The normal equations for quadratic least squares regression are given by:

$\begin{pmatrix} \sum x^4 & \sum x^3 & \sum x^2 \\ \sum x^3 & \sum x^2 & \sum x \\ \sum x^2 & \sum x & n \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \sum x^2 y \\ \sum xy \\ \sum y \end{pmatrix} \implies \begin{pmatrix} 2275 & 441 & 91 \\ 441 & 91 & 21 \\ 91 & 21 & 6 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1430.5 \\ 283.3 \\ 61.6 \end{pmatrix}$

Solving this non-singular symmetric linear system yields exact coefficients:

$a = \mathbf{0.5982}, \quad b = \mathbf{-0.3189}, \quad c = \mathbf{2.3100}$
Optimal Parabolic Fit: $y = 0.5982x^2 - 0.3189x + 2.3100 \quad$ ($\text{RSS} = \mathbf{0.0509}, \; R^2 = \mathbf{0.9998}$)

4.3 Curve Model 3: Exponential Fit ($y = a e^{bx}$)

Transforming non-linear exponential dynamics via natural logarithm: $\ln(y) = \ln(a) + bx \implies Y = A + bx$, where $Y = \ln(y)$ and $A = \ln(a)$. The linear normal equations in transformed coordinates are:

$\sum Y = nA + b\sum x \implies 6A + 21.0b = 12.4580$
$\sum xY = A\sum x + b\sum x^2 \implies 21.0A + 91.0b = 51.2253$

Solving: $\Delta = 105$. $b = \frac{6(51.2253) - 21.0(12.4580)}{105} = \frac{307.3518 - 261.6180}{105} = \mathbf{0.4356}$.

$A = \frac{12.4580 - 21.0(0.4356)}{6} = \mathbf{0.5519} \implies a = e^{A} = e^{0.5519} = \mathbf{1.7365}$.

Optimal Exponential Fit: $y = 1.7365 \, e^{0.4356x} \quad$ ($\text{RSS} = 3.4720$)

4.4 Curve Model 4: Power Law Fit ($y = a x^b$)

Taking logarithms on both sides: $\ln(y) = \ln(a) + b\ln(x) \implies Y = A + bX$, where $X = \ln(x), Y = \ln(y)$.

Evaluating summations: $\sum X = 6.5793, \sum X^2 = 9.4099, \sum XY = 16.3203$. Determinant $\Delta = 6(9.4099) - (6.5793)^2 = 13.1722$.

$b = \frac{6(16.3203) - (6.5793)(12.4580)}{13.1722} = \frac{97.9218 - 81.9649}{13.1722} = \mathbf{1.2114}$
$A = \frac{12.4580 - (1.2114)(6.5793)}{6} = \mathbf{0.7480} \implies a = e^{0.7480} = \mathbf{2.1127}$

$r = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x\sum y}{\sqrt{[n\sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2][n\sum y^2 - (\sum y)^2]}} = \frac{6(283.3) - (21.0)(61.6)}{\sqrt{[6(91) - 441][6(907.74) - (61.6)^2]}} = \frac{406.2}{\sqrt{105 \times 1651.88}} = \mathbf{0.975340}$

The sample means are $\bar{x} = \frac{21.0}{6} = 3.5000$ and $\bar{y} = \frac{61.6}{6} = 10.2667$. The regression coefficients are:

Regression of $y$ on $x$: $b_{yx} = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x\sum y}{n\sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2} = \frac{406.2}{105} = \mathbf{3.8686}$
Regression of $x$ on $y$: $b_{xy} = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x\sum y}{n\sum y^2 - (\sum y)^2} = \frac{406.2}{1651.88} = \mathbf{0.245902}$

Consistency Check: $r = \sqrt{b_{yx} \cdot b_{xy}} = \sqrt{3.8686 \times 0.245902} = \sqrt{0.951296} = \mathbf{0.975344}$, verifying perfect mathematical symmetry.

7. Full-Range Fourier Expansions for Periodic Phenomena

Actuators, motor drives, and resonant transducers in smart systems operate under non-sinusoidal periodic command signals. By Dirichlet's conditions, any single-valued, bounded periodic function $f(t)$ with a finite number of discontinuities can be decomposed into an infinite series of orthogonal harmonics.

7.1 Full-Range Fourier Series over Standard Period ($2\pi$): $f(t) = t^2$ in $(-\pi, \pi)$

Consider the symmetric quadratic acceleration command profile $f(t) = t^2$ defined over $(-\pi, \pi)$ with period $T = 2\pi$. Since $f(-t) = (-t)^2 = t^2$, $f(t)$ is an even function. Thus, all sine coefficients vanish identically: $b_n = 0$.

$a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t) dt = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi t^2 dt = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ \frac{t^3}{3} \right]_0^\pi = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi^3}{3} = \mathbf{\frac{2\pi^2}{3}}$

Applying Bernoulli's Generalized Integration by Parts for $a_n$:

$a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi t^2 \cos(nt) dt = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ t^2 \left(\frac{\sin nt}{n}\right) - (2t) \left(-\frac{\cos nt}{n^2}\right) + (2) \left(-\frac{\sin nt}{n^3}\right) \right]_0^\pi$
$a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ 0 + \frac{2\pi \cos(n\pi)}{n^2} - 0 \right] = \frac{2}{\pi} \left( \frac{2\pi (-1)^n}{n^2} \right) = \mathbf{\frac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}}$

Substituting $a_0$ and $a_n$ into the Fourier synthesis formula $f(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n \cos(nt)$:

$\mathbf{t^2 = \frac{\pi^2}{3} + 4 \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n}{n^2} \cos(nt) = \frac{\pi^2}{3} - 4\left(\frac{\cos t}{1^2} - \frac{\cos 2t}{2^2} + \frac{\cos 3t}{3^2} - \dots\right)}$
Harmonic Series Deductions:
1. Setting $t = 0$: $0 = \frac{\pi^2}{3} - 4\left(1 - \frac{1}{2^2} + \frac{1}{3^2} - \dots\right) \implies \mathbf{\frac{1}{1^2} - \frac{1}{2^2} + \frac{1}{3^2} - \frac{1}{4^2} + \dots = \frac{\pi^2}{12}} \approx 0.82247$
2. Setting $t = \pi$: $\pi^2 = \frac{\pi^2}{3} + 4\left(\frac{1}{1^2} + \frac{1}{2^2} + \frac{1}{3^2} + \dots\right) \implies \mathbf{\frac{1}{1^2} + \frac{1}{2^2} + \frac{1}{3^2} + \dots = \frac{\pi^2}{6}} \approx 1.64493$

7.2 Full-Range Fourier Series over Arbitrary Period ($2L$): Triangular Signal $f(t) = |t|$ in $(-2, 2)$

In pulse-width modulation (PWM) power electronics, arbitrary-period carrier signals are ubiquitous. Consider the symmetric triangular wave $f(t) = |t|$ on $(-2, 2)$ with half-period $L = 2$ (fundamental period $T = 2L = 4$).

Since $f(-t) = |-t| = |t| = f(t)$, the signal is strictly even, implying $b_n = 0$. The coefficients are:

$a_0 = \frac{2}{L} \int_0^L f(t) dt = \frac{2}{2} \int_0^2 t dt = \left[ \frac{t^2}{2} \right]_0^2 = \mathbf{2}$
$a_n = \frac{2}{L} \int_0^L f(t) \cos\left(\frac{n\pi t}{L}\right) dt = \int_0^2 t \cos\left(\frac{n\pi t}{2}\right) dt = \left[ t \frac{2}{n\pi} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi t}{2}\right) - (1) \left(-\frac{4}{n^2\pi^2}\cos\left(\frac{n\pi t}{2}\right)\right) \right]_0^2$
$a_n = 0 + \frac{4}{n^2\pi^2} [\cos(n\pi) - \cos(0)] = \mathbf{\frac{4}{n^2\pi^2} [(-1)^n - 1]} = \begin{cases} \mathbf{-\frac{8}{n^2\pi^2}}, & n = 1, 3, 5, \dots \text{ (odd)} \\ \mathbf{0}, & n = 2, 4, 6, \dots \text{ (even)} \end{cases}$
Synthesized Arbitrary Period Fourier Series: $$\mathbf{f(t) = 1 - \frac{8}{\pi^2} \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{(2k-1)^2} \cos\left(\frac{(2k-1)\pi t}{2}\right) = 1 - \frac{8}{\pi^2} \left[ \cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) + \frac{1}{9}\cos\left(\frac{3\pi t}{2}\right) + \frac{1}{25}\cos\left(\frac{5\pi t}{2}\right) + \dots \right]}$$

Convergence Analysis: The harmonic amplitudes decay as $\mathcal{O}(1/n^2)$, ensuring rapid mathematical convergence. The fundamental frequency component carries $\frac{8}{\pi^2} \approx 81.06\%$ of the total alternating signal energy.

8. Half-Range Expansions for Sensor Ramping Transients

In many smart control systems, sensor calibration ramps and valve opening commands are observed only over the finite interval $(0, L)$. Depending on whether an odd or even periodic extension is constructed, the function is expanded as either a pure Sine or pure Cosine half-range series.

8.1 Half-Range Sine Series (Odd Extension)

For a linear ramp $f(t) = kt$ over $(0, L)$, extending oddly makes $a_0 = 0$ and $a_n = 0$:

$b_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L kt \sin\left(\frac{n\pi t}{L}\right) dt$
$b_n = \frac{2k}{L} \left[ -t\frac{L}{n\pi}\cos\frac{n\pi t}{L} + \frac{L^2}{n^2\pi^2}\sin\frac{n\pi t}{L} \right]_0^L$
$b_n = -\frac{2kL}{n\pi} \cos(n\pi) = \mathbf{\frac{2kL}{n\pi} (-1)^{n+1}}$
Sine Series Expansion:
$f(t) = \frac{2kL}{\pi} \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi t}{L}\right)$

8.2 Half-Range Cosine Series (Even Extension)

Extending evenly over $(-L, L)$ sets $b_n = 0$:

$a_0 = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L kt dt = \mathbf{kL}$
$a_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L kt \cos\left(\frac{n\pi t}{L}\right) dt$
$a_n = \frac{2kL}{n^2\pi^2} [(-1)^n - 1] = \begin{cases} \mathbf{-\frac{4kL}{n^2\pi^2}}, & n \text{ odd} \\ \mathbf{0}, & n \text{ even} \end{cases}$
Cosine Series Expansion:
$f(t) = \frac{kL}{2} - \frac{4kL}{\pi^2}\sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{\cos\left(\frac{(2m-1)\pi t}{L}\right)}{(2m-1)^2}$

9. Practical Harmonic Analysis Formulation (Tabulated Discrete Field Data)

In operating smart microgrids and transducer buses, analytical equations $f(t)$ are unavailable; instead, discrete telemetry values $y_0, y_1, \dots, y_{N-1}$ are recorded at $N$ equidistant intervals over a full period $T = 2\pi$. Practical Harmonic Analysis (Numerical Fourier Integration) computes the continuous Fourier coefficients via finite summation averages:

$\text{Mean / DC Value: } \frac{a_0}{2} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} y_i \implies a_0 = \frac{2}{N}\sum y$
$a_n = \frac{2}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} y_i \cos(n\theta_i), \qquad b_n = \frac{2}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} y_i \sin(n\theta_i)$

The signal is reconstructed up to the $k^{\text{th}}$ harmonic as:

$y(\theta) \approx \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^k [a_n \cos(n\theta) + b_n \sin(n\theta)] = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^k c_n \sin(n\theta + \phi_n)$

where $c_n = \sqrt{a_n^2 + b_n^2}$ represents the harmonic amplitude and $\phi_n = \text{atan2}(a_n, b_n)$ represents the phase angle.

10. Continuous Fourier Transforms for Non-Periodic Transients

Unlike steady-state cyclic signals, non-periodic operational transientsâ€”such as electromagnetic interference (EMI) pulses, lightning surges, and actuator start-up surgesâ€”cannot be represented by discrete Fourier series. Continuous Fourier transforms map non-periodic time signals into the continuous frequency spectrum:

$\mathcal{F}\{f(t)\} = F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t) e^{-i\omega t} dt, \qquad \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{F(\omega)\} = f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty F(\omega) e^{i\omega t} d\omega$

10.1 Derivation for Double-Sided Exponential Surge: $f(t) = e^{-a|t|}$ ($a > 0$)

Splitting the infinite integral across negative and positive time domains:

$F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^0 e^{at} e^{-i\omega t} dt + \int_0^\infty e^{-at} e^{-i\omega t} dt = \int_{-\infty}^0 e^{(a - i\omega)t} dt + \int_0^\infty e^{-(a + i\omega)t} dt$
$F(\omega) = \left[ \frac{e^{(a - i\omega)t}}{a - i\omega} \right]_{-\infty}^0 + \left[ \frac{e^{-(a + i\omega)t}}{-(a + i\omega)} \right]_0^\infty = \left( \frac{1}{a - i\omega} - 0 \right) + \left( 0 + \frac{1}{a + i\omega} \right)$
$F(\omega) = \frac{(a + i\omega) + (a - i\omega)}{(a - i\omega)(a + i\omega)} = \mathbf{\frac{2a}{a^2 + \omega^2}}$
Spectral Fourier Transform: $F(\omega) = \frac{2a}{a^2 + \omega^2} \quad$ (Lorentzian low-pass continuous frequency spectrum)

10.2 Rigorous Proof & Verification of Parseval's Energy Theorem

Parseval's identity states that total signal energy is invariant across the time and frequency domains:

$\int_{-\infty}^\infty |f(t)|^2 dt = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty |F(\omega)|^2 d\omega$

LHS Evaluation (Time Domain Energy):

$E_{\text{time}} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty [e^{-a|t|}]^2 dt = 2 \int_0^\infty e^{-2at} dt = 2 \left[ \frac{e^{-2at}}{-2a} \right]_0^\infty = 2 \left( 0 - \left(-\frac{1}{2a}\right) \right) = \mathbf{\frac{1}{a}}$

RHS Evaluation (Frequency Domain Energy):

$E_{\text{freq}} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left(\frac{2a}{a^2 + \omega^2}\right)^2 d\omega = \frac{4a^2}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{d\omega}{(a^2 + \omega^2)^2} = \frac{4a^2}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{d\omega}{(a^2 + \omega^2)^2}$

Substitute $\omega = a\tan\theta \implies d\omega = a\sec^2\theta d\theta$, with limits $\theta \in [0, \pi/2]$:

$\int_0^\infty \frac{d\omega}{(a^2 + \omega^2)^2} = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{a\sec^2\theta d\theta}{[a^2(1 + \tan^2\theta)]^2} = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{a\sec^2\theta d\theta}{a^4\sec^4\theta} = \frac{1}{a^3}\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^2\theta d\theta = \frac{1}{a^3} \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{4a^3}$

Multiplying by the prefactor: $E_{\text{freq}} = \frac{4a^2}{\pi} \left( \frac{\pi}{4a^3} \right) = \mathbf{\frac{1}{a}}$.

Parseval's Verification: $E_{\text{time}} = \frac{1}{a} \equiv E_{\text{freq}} = \frac{1}{a}$. The equality holds identically, proving complete energy conservation across the signal domain.

10.3 Fourier Sine and Cosine Transforms of Causal Exponential Pulse $f(t) = e^{-at}$ ($t > 0$)

Fourier Sine Transform:
$\mathcal{F}_s\{e^{-at}\} = \int_0^\infty e^{-at}\sin(\omega t) dt = \mathbf{\frac{\omega}{a^2 + \omega^2}}$
Behavior: Reaches maximum at $\omega = a$; zero DC transmission ($F_s(0) = 0$).
Fourier Cosine Transform:
$\mathcal{F}_c\{e^{-at}\} = \int_0^\infty e^{-at}\cos(\omega t) dt = \mathbf{\frac{a}{a^2 + \omega^2}}$
Behavior: Maximum at $\omega = 0$ ($F_c(0) = 1/a$); monotonic low-pass roll-off.

Application to Signal Filtering: The continuous spectrum confirms that non-periodic noise attenuation occurs at high frequencies with a roll-off of $-20\text{ dB/decade}$. The $-3\text{dB}$ half-power cutoff frequency is $\omega_c = a$.

11. Stochastic Failure & Reliability Engineering

Real-world smart automated systems are subject to hardware component wear-out, environmental voltage transients, and analog Gaussian measurement noise. Comprehensive operational risk is modeled using four standard distributions:

11.1 Binomial Distribution: Multi-Node Sensor Redundancy

An array of $n = 8$ redundant smart sensor nodes operates in a harsh field environment. The probability of an individual node failing during an operating cycle is $p = 0.05$ ($q = 1 - p = 0.95$). Let $X \sim B(n=8, p=0.05)$ be the number of failed nodes:

$P(X = k) = \binom{8}{k} (0.05)^k (0.95)^{8-k}$
$P(X = 0) = (0.95)^8 = \mathbf{0.663420}, \quad P(X = 1) = 8(0.05)(0.95)^7 = \mathbf{0.279335}, \quad P(X = 2) = 28(0.05)^2(0.95)^6 = \mathbf{0.051456}$
Voting Cluster Reliability (At most 1 failure): $P(X \le 1) = 0.663420 + 0.279335 = \mathbf{0.942755} \quad (94.28\%)$
Catastrophic Redundancy Failure ($>2$ failures): $P(X > 2) = 1 - [P(0)+P(1)+P(2)] = \mathbf{0.005788} \quad (0.58\%)$

11.2 Poisson Distribution: Transient Electrical Fault Arrivals

Operational transient electrical noise spikes occur randomly at an average rate of $\lambda = 2.4$ events per 100 operating hours. The probability of encountering $k$ spikes is governed by $P(X = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!}$:

$P(X = 0) = e^{-2.4} = \mathbf{0.090718}, \quad P(X = 1) = \frac{e^{-2.4}(2.4)^1}{1!} = \mathbf{0.217723}, \quad P(X = 2) = \frac{e^{-2.4}(2.4)^2}{2!} = \mathbf{0.261268}$
$P(X = 3) = \frac{e^{-2.4}(2.4)^3}{6} = \mathbf{0.209014}, \quad \mathbf{P(X \le 2) = 0.090718 + 0.217723 + 0.261268 = 0.569709} \quad (56.97\%)$

11.3 Exponential Distribution: Actuator Lifetime & MTBF

The time-to-failure $T$ of a smart linear actuator exhibits constant failure rate $\lambda = 0.0005\text{ hr}^{-1}$, corresponding to a Mean Time Between Failures $\text{MTBF} = 1/\lambda = \mathbf{2000\text{ hours}}$. The reliability function $R(t) = P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}$ evaluates to:

$R(500\text{ h}) = e^{-0.0005(500)} = e^{-0.25} = \mathbf{0.778801} \quad (77.88\%)$
$R(1000\text{ h}) = e^{-0.0005(1000)} = e^{-0.50} = \mathbf{0.606531} \quad (60.65\%)$
$R(2000\text{ h}) = e^{-0.0005(2000)} = e^{-1.00} = \mathbf{0.367879} \quad (36.79\% \text{ survival at MTBF})$

11.4 Normal (Gaussian) Distribution: Sensor Signal Measurement Noise

The calibrated regulated sensor output voltage $X$ exhibits Gaussian thermal noise: $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu = 5.00\text{ V}, \sigma = 0.08\text{ V})$. The acceptable engineering tolerance band is $[4.80\text{ V}, 5.20\text{ V}]$. Transforming to standard normal $Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$:

$Z_{\text{lower}} = \frac{4.80 - 5.00}{0.08} = \mathbf{-2.50}, \qquad Z_{\text{upper}} = \frac{5.20 - 5.00}{0.08} = \mathbf{+2.50}$
$P(4.80 \le X \le 5.20) = \Phi(2.50) - \Phi(-2.50) = 0.993790 - 0.006210 = \mathbf{0.987581} \quad (\mathbf{98.76\%} \text{ within spec})$
$P(\text{Out of Spec}) = 1 - 0.987581 = \mathbf{0.012419} \quad (\mathbf{12,419.4\text{ PPM defect rate}})$

13.1 Stochastic Transition Probability Matrix ($P$) & Higher-Order Powers

The 1-step transition probability matrix $P = [p_{ij}]$ is defined such that row sums equal 1 ($\sum_j p_{ij} = 1$):

$P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.85 & 0.12 & 0.03 \\ 0.20 & 0.70 & 0.10 \\ 0.40 & 0.30 & 0.30 \end{pmatrix}$

Higher-order transition matrices $P^2$ and $P^3$ are evaluated via matrix multiplication:

$P^2 = P \cdot P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7585 & 0.1950 & 0.0465 \\ 0.3500 & 0.5440 & 0.1060 \\ 0.5200 & 0.3480 & 0.1320 \end{pmatrix}$
$P^3 = P^2 \cdot P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7023 & 0.2415 & 0.0562 \\ 0.4487 & 0.4546 & 0.0967 \\ 0.5644 & 0.3456 & 0.0900 \end{pmatrix}$

13.2 Closed-Form Derivation of Long-Term Steady-State Probabilities ($\mathbf{\pi}$)

Since all entries of $P^2$ are strictly positive ($p_{ij}^{(2)} > 0$), the Markov chain is regular and ergodic. The stationary probability vector $\mathbf{\pi} = [\pi_1, \pi_2, \pi_3]$ satisfies the balance equations $\mathbf{\pi} P = \mathbf{\pi}$ subject to $\sum \pi_i = 1$:

(1) $0.85\pi_1 + 0.20\pi_2 + 0.40\pi_3 = \pi_1 \implies -0.15\pi_1 + 0.20\pi_2 + 0.40\pi_3 = 0$
(2) $0.12\pi_1 + 0.70\pi_2 + 0.30\pi_3 = \pi_2 \implies 0.12\pi_1 - 0.30\pi_2 + 0.30\pi_3 = 0$
(3) $\pi_1 + \pi_2 + \pi_3 = 1$

From equation (2): $0.12\pi_1 = 0.30\pi_2 - 0.30\pi_3 \implies \pi_1 = 2.5\pi_2 - 2.5\pi_3$. Substituting into equation (1):

$-0.15(2.5\pi_2 - 2.5\pi_3) + 0.20\pi_2 + 0.40\pi_3 = 0 \implies -0.375\pi_2 + 0.375\pi_3 + 0.20\pi_2 + 0.40\pi_3 = 0$
$-0.175\pi_2 + 0.775\pi_3 = 0 \implies \pi_2 = \frac{0.775}{0.175}\pi_3 = \frac{31}{7}\pi_3$

Expressing $\pi_1$ in terms of $\pi_3$: $\pi_1 = 2.5\left(\frac{31}{7}\pi_3\right) - 2.5\pi_3 = 2.5\left(\frac{24}{7}\right)\pi_3 = \frac{60}{7}\pi_3$. Substituting into (3):

$\frac{60}{7}\pi_3 + \frac{31}{7}\pi_3 + \frac{7}{7}\pi_3 = 1 \implies \frac{98}{7}\pi_3 = 1 \implies 14\pi_3 = 1 \implies \mathbf{\pi_3 = \frac{1}{14} = \frac{7}{98} \approx 0.071429}$
$\mathbf{\pi_1 = \frac{60}{98} = \frac{30}{49} \approx 0.612245}, \qquad \mathbf{\pi_2 = \frac{31}{98} \approx 0.316327}$
Exact Steady-State Vector: $\mathbf{\pi = [0.612245, \; 0.316327, \; 0.071429]} = \mathbf{[61.22\%, \; 31.63\%, \; 7.14\%]}$
Operational Availability: $A = \pi_1 + \pi_2 = 61.22\% + 31.63\% = \mathbf{92.86\%}$ operational uptime.
Mean Recurrence Times ($\mu_{ii} = 1/\pi_i$): $\mu_{11} = \frac{49}{30} = \mathbf{1.63\text{ steps}}, \; \mu_{22} = \frac{98}{31} = \mathbf{3.16\text{ steps}}, \; \mu_{33} = 14 = \mathbf{14.00\text{ steps}}$.

17. Technical Viva-Voce Questions & Model Answers

Q1: Why did the parabolic fit achieve a much lower RSS (0.0509) than the linear model (13.4110)?
Ans: The transducer's underlying physical sensor mechanism exhibits second-order non-linearities. The quadratic curvature term $ax^2$ ($a = 0.5982$) matches empirical telemetry, yielding $R^2 = 0.9998$.

Q2: How does Practical Harmonic Analysis differ from continuous Fourier analytical integration?
Ans: Continuous Fourier integration requires an explicit closed-form $f(t)$. Practical Harmonic Analysis applies discrete numerical quadrature over tabulated equidistant measurements: $a_n = \frac{2}{N}\sum y\cos(n\theta)$.

Q3: How was Parseval's identity verified for $f(t) = e^{-a|t|}$?
Ans: The time-domain integral $\int_{-\infty}^\infty [e^{-a|t|}]^2 dt = \frac{1}{a}$ identically matches the frequency integral $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{4a^2}{(a^2+\omega^2)^2} d\omega = \frac{1}{a}$, proving energy conservation.

Q4: Why is the Markov chain steady-state probability for failure only $\pi_3 = 7.14\%$?
Ans: Because of strong self-retention in the normal state ($p_{11} = 0.85$) and high automated recovery transitions from outage back to normal ($p_{31} = 0.40$), yielding an operational availability of $92.86\%$.
